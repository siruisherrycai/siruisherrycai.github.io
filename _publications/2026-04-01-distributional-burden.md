Risk Rating 2.0 replaced the National Flood Insurance Program's zone-based premiums with
property-level actuarial rates. Linking NFIP policy microdata with ACS demographics and
ZTRAX transactions for Florida, we study how this repricing affects both recurring ownership
costs and residential transaction prices.

Upward repricing is more common in higher-income tracts and slightly larger in dollar terms,
yet the premium burden is sharply regressive: bottom-quintile tracts face premium-to-income
ratios 2.5 times those of top-quintile tracts, a gap driven by income scaling rather than
larger dollar increases. A two-phase difference-in-differences design comparing
positive-repricing tracts with flat or downward-repricing tracts shows that sale prices fall
by about 2 percent, reaching 2.6 percent after the renewal phase, with capitalization
concentrated in low-income tracts and near zero in high-income tracts.

In a 30-year accounting exercise, combining the premium stream with the estimated
mark-to-market price discount implies a combined homeowner exposure that is substantially
larger, as a share of annual household income, for bottom-quintile policyholders than for
top-quintile policyholders. Although upward repricing is concentrated in higher-income
tracts, the income-scaled burden and price discounts fall most heavily on lower-income
markets.
